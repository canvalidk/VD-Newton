# The Six-Entry Functional Unit of a Valid Dictionary Law

## The Triplet Is Necessary but Not Sufficient

The minimal graph-theoretic feature of a law in a Valid Dictionary is the triplet — a 3-cycle in the dependency graph where three entries mutually define one another. But the triplet alone is not a self-contained functional unit. It takes **six entries** to make a law fully operational within the dictionary. The triplet is the core, but it needs three companion entries to connect it to the rest of the log.

## Two Definitions Per Headword

Each of the three headwords involved in a law receives two entries — two separate definitions at different points in the log. These serve fundamentally different roles:

**The peripheral definition** tells you what the word *is* — how it connects outward to concepts already established elsewhere in the dictionary. It anchors the headword in the broader web of prior definitions.

**The triplet definition** tells you how the word relates to the other two members of the law. Specifically, it characterizes how the two *daughter* ideas connect *through* this headword. It faces inward, toward the law's internal structure.

A crucial subtlety: each triplet definition at a given corner expresses how two daughter ideas relate to the headword, but it does *not* directly express how those two daughters relate to each other. That relationship is emergent — it arises from the intersection of all three triplet definitions taken together. No single entry states it outright.

## The Entry Order: Chimney, Triplet, Walls

The six entries must appear in the log in a specific order, constrained by the append-only rule and the requirement that exactly one of the three headwords is previously defined (the November word) while the other two are born in the triplet (the winter words).

**Step 1 — The Chimney (1 entry).** A peripheral definition for the November word. This entry defines the headword in standalone terms, connecting it to prior dictionary content. It must appear before the triplet so that the November word counts as "previously defined" when the triplet is laid down.

**Step 2 — The Triplet (3 entries).** The three law entries. The November word is redefined in terms of the two winter words. The two winter words are each defined for the first time, each characterizing how the other two ideas meet at that corner. After this step, all three headwords have their inward-facing, relational definitions.

**Step 3 — The Walls (2 entries).** Peripheral definitions for the two winter words. These entries give the winter words their outward-facing definitions — what they *are* in terms of the broader dictionary, independent of the specific law that introduced them. These entries connect the newly born concepts to the rest of the log so that future entries can reference them.

## The House with a Chimney

When you trace the dependency arrows between these six entries, the shape they form resembles a house with a chimney. The chimney is the single peripheral entry at the top, feeding down into the apex of the triplet. The triplet occupies the middle — the roof of the house. The two wall entries sit at the base, grounding the structure.

The arrows between entries don't all represent the same kind of relationship. Some are "same headword, different definition" links (vertical: chimney to its triplet entry, triplet entries down to their wall entries). Others are the mutual-definition links within the triplet itself (the 3-cycle). The house shape captures both kinds simultaneously.

## Why the Six-Entry Unit Is Hard to See

In a working dictionary, laws are not produced in isolation. They are laid down consecutively, and their six-entry units **overlap**. The wall entries from one law become the chimney entry for the next. A peripheral definition does double duty — it is simultaneously the outward-facing grounding of a winter word from the previous law and the previously-defined anchor for the next law's November word.

This overlap means that when you read the log sequentially, the three-entry triplet pattern is easy to spot — it repeats with regular spacing. But the six-entry functional unit is hidden, because its boundaries are interleaved with adjacent units. You see the triplets clearly; the houses are smeared across them.

Understanding the dictionary's architecture requires seeing through the three-entry surface pattern to the six-entry deep structure underneath.
